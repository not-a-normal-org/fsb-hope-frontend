Tokyo is one of the most rewarding award routes flying out of the US, and one of the most contested. The lie-flat seats people actually want to sit in (ANA's "The Room," Japan Airlines' Sky Suite) get redeemed fast, and the programs that price them cheaply are not always the programs you have points in. Booking this route well is less about finding a secret rate and more about knowing which door to walk through, and when.

Here is how the US–Tokyo business-class map looks at the time of writing, and where the value actually sits.

## The two seats worth chasing

For nonstop US–Japan business class, two carriers dominate the wish list: **ANA** and **Japan Airlines (JAL)**. Both fly from the major US gateways (JFK, EWR, LAX, SFO, plus Chicago, Dallas, and others) into Narita (NRT) and Haneda (HND).

You rarely book these seats with the airline's own currency directly. You book them by transferring flexible credit-card points into a partner program that prices the seat well and can see the award space. That partner choice is the whole game. Start with our [foundation guide to flying on credit-card points](/blog/how-to-fly-for-free-with-credit-card-points), then come back, because the rest of this assumes you know the basics.

## Reaching ANA business class

ANA is a Star Alliance carrier, which opens a few paths. Two matter most for US travelers.

**Amex Membership Rewards to Virgin Atlantic Flying Club.** Amex transfers to Virgin at 1:1, and Virgin's partner chart prices ANA business at roughly **52,500 points one-way from the West Coast and around 60,000 from the East Coast** at the time of writing. That is one of the strongest rates on the route. The catch: you cannot book ANA on Virgin's website. You find saver space through another Star Alliance program's search, then call Virgin Atlantic to ticket it. Virgin also passes through ANA's fuel surcharges, so budget a few hundred dollars in taxes and fees.

**Amex Membership Rewards to ANA Mileage Club.** Amex is the primary US transfer partner into ANA's own program, at 1:1, though transfers are not instant, so allow roughly two days. ANA's own award chart is inexpensive, historically round-trip only; ANA introduced limited one-way awards in 2025, but many redemptions still assume a round trip, so plan the return leg before you commit. ANA's awards also carry meaningful fuel surcharges.

United MileagePlus can also ticket ANA, but United prices international partner awards dynamically, and business class to Tokyo often lands well above the fixed-chart rates above. It is a fallback, not a first choice.

## Reaching JAL business class

JAL sits in the **oneworld** alliance, and two programs stand out.

**American AAdvantage.** JAL business from the US to Tokyo has historically priced around **60,000 AAdvantage miles one-way with very low taxes**, on the order of a few dollars, because American does not pass through JAL's fuel surcharges. That combination of a fair rate and near-zero fees makes it one of the cleanest redemptions on the route. Note that American has been nudging some connecting itineraries higher (toward 80,000 miles), so the nonstop, single-segment rate is the one to target.

**Alaska Mileage Plan (now Atmos Rewards).** Alaska has long published a low JAL business rate, in the **55,000–60,000 range one-way** with modest fees. The trade-off is that Alaska's award space on JAL is thin and can be hard to surface, and the program's transfer-partner landscape has shifted. It rewards patience more than most.

## What the numbers actually look like

Rough, evergreen ranges for **one-way US–Tokyo business class** at the time of writing:

- **ANA via Virgin Atlantic:** ~52,500–60,000 points, plus fuel surcharges
- **ANA via ANA Mileage Club:** low fixed-chart pricing, plus fuel surcharges, often round-trip framed
- **JAL via American AAdvantage:** ~60,000 miles, minimal taxes
- **JAL via Alaska/Atmos:** ~55,000–60,000 miles, modest fees, scarce space

Treat these as reference points, not live quotes. Award pricing, transfer ratios, and surcharge levels move, sometimes with little notice, and periodic transfer bonuses (Amex to Virgin has run 30% bonuses) can shift the math further. Always confirm the current rate before you transfer, because **transfers are one-way and usually irreversible**.

## The watch-outs that quietly cost you

The rate is the easy part. These are the traps:

- **Fuel surcharges.** The same ANA seat can cost you a clean set of taxes through one program and hundreds of dollars in surcharges through another. Which door you pick changes the out-of-pocket, not just the points. This is worth understanding in its own right; see [how to avoid fuel surcharges on award flights](/blog/avoid-fuel-surcharges-award-flights).
- **Transfer timing.** Some transfers are instant; ANA's are not. If space is sitting there and you transfer a currency that takes two days to arrive, the seat can be gone before your points land. You confirm the space *first*, then move points, never the reverse.
- **Round-trip framing.** ANA's structure has historically assumed a round trip. Build your return into the plan rather than discovering the constraint after you have committed points.
- **Award release timing.** ANA and JAL release long-haul space on their own cadence, often a chunk far in advance, plus scattered seats closer to departure. Two travelers who need to sit together make this materially harder.
- **The right currency for the seat.** Having Amex points does not mean Virgin is your best route on a given date, and knowing all of this is why picking the transfer partner matters more than picking the airline.
