Transferable points are the most flexible currency in the miles world, and the one beginners misuse most. The mistake is almost always the same: moving points to an airline before there's a seat to book. Transfers are usually irreversible. Get the order wrong and you can strand tens of thousands of points in a program you never meant to use.

This guide explains what transferable points are, how partner transfers actually work, and the single discipline that separates a clean redemption from a costly one.

## What "transferable points" actually means

Five major card currencies are transferable: American Express Membership Rewards, Chase Ultimate Rewards, Citi ThankYou, Capital One Miles, and Bilt Rewards. What makes them powerful is that they don't lock you into one airline. Instead, each one partners with a roster of airline and hotel loyalty programs, and you move points into whichever partner prices your specific flight best.

That flexibility is the whole point. A fixed-value program ties your balance to one airline's award chart. Transferable points let you shop across programs, booking the same physical seat through whichever partner charges the fewest miles. If you're new to the mechanics of earning these points in the first place, start with our [foundation guide to flying on credit card points](/blog/how-to-fly-for-free-with-credit-card-points), then come back here for the transfer step.

The other thing worth understanding early: a transferable balance is worth more than the airline miles it can become, precisely because you haven't committed it yet. The moment you transfer, you trade optionality for a specific award. That trade is often the right one, but it should be made deliberately rather than out of impatience.

## How a transfer works, and the ratios that matter

A transfer moves points out of your card account and into an airline or hotel program as that program's own miles. Most transferable currencies move to their major airline partners at **1:1**: 1,000 card points become 1,000 airline miles. That's the baseline you should expect, and it's why these currencies are valued the way they are.

But 1:1 is not universal, and the exceptions cost real value. At the time of writing:

- **Chase Ultimate Rewards** moves to its airline partners at 1:1, in 1,000-point increments. The fine print now lives in its hotel partners: the flagship 1:1 rate to World of Hyatt holds on the top-tier Sapphire Reserve, while the mid-tier Sapphire Preferred and Ink Business Preferred have moved to 4:3 for that same partner. Same points, same hotel program, a quarter of the value lost on the wrong card.
- **American Express Membership Rewards** is mostly 1:1, but several partners are worse: Cathay Pacific and Emirates transfer at 5:4, JetBlue at roughly 1:0.8, and Hilton at a very different 1:2.
- **Capital One Miles** are largely 1:1, with exceptions such as EVA Air at 4:3.
- **Bilt Rewards** transfers at 1:1 to its airline and hotel partners.
- **Citi ThankYou** ratios depend on which card you hold: premium cards transfer at full value, while some no-annual-fee cards transfer at a reduced 10:7.

Two lessons follow. First, **never assume the ratio**. Check it for your exact card and partner before you move anything, because a 5:4 or 10:7 rate quietly erases 20–30% of your balance. Second, partner rosters and ratios change, and the Chase-to-Hyatt cut above is a live example of a rate that was 1:1 for years and no longer is on every card. Treat every list, including this one, as a snapshot to reconfirm at the moment you book.

## The golden rule: find the seat first, then transfer

This is the rule the entire practice rests on: **confirm the award seat is available and bookable before you transfer a single point.**

Award space is finite and volatile. An airline releases a limited number of seats at the saver price, and they can vanish between the moment you see them and the moment you're ready to book. If you transfer speculatively, moving points because a fare *looks* achievable, or because a transfer bonus is ending, you're betting on a seat you haven't secured. When the seat is gone, your points are stranded in an airline program with no easy way back. If you've ever watched availability evaporate mid-search, our piece on [phantom award space and why seats vanish](/blog/phantom-award-space-why-seats-vanish) explains what's happening behind the screen.

The correct sequence is narrow and unforgiving:

1. Find the specific award seat and confirm it's live and bookable.
2. Verify the exact point cost and the transfer ratio for your card.
3. Transfer only the amount that flight requires.
4. Book immediately, while the seat is still there.

Transfers are almost always **one-way and irreversible**. There is no "undo," no customer-service reversal, no transfer back to your card. Once Membership Rewards become airline miles, they follow that airline's rules, its award chart, and its expiration policy, permanently. ## Transfer time is a booking risk, not a footnote

Not all transfers are instant, and speed matters more than people expect. Some partners post the miles in seconds; others take hours, and a few take one to several days.

At the time of writing, the pattern looks roughly like this, though any program can change its processing without notice:

- **Instant, or close to it:** most airline partners, including British Airways, Air Canada Aeroplan, Virgin Atlantic, Flying Blue, and United. These let you find space and book in a single sitting.
- **Slow, plan ahead:** ANA Mileage Club, Singapore KrisFlyer, and Iberia are the usual exceptions, with transfers that can take a day or two to post.

That lag is a risk, because a seat you confirmed can disappear during a slow transfer. If you find award space with a partner that transfers instantly, you can move points and book with confidence. If the partner is a slow one, the seat may not survive the wait, and sometimes the right move is a different partner that prices the same flight and transfers instantly.

This is exactly where speculative transfers punish people. Moving points "to be ready" assumes the seat waits for you. It rarely does. The only safe transfer is one made against a seat you've already confirmed.

## A closer look at where transfers pay off

The reason to learn this machinery is that specific partners price specific routes far below their peers. A few come up again and again for good reason, and they illustrate why the same points can be worth wildly different things depending on where you send them.

- **Air Canada Aeroplan** is a favorite because its award structure permits a stopover on one-way redemptions, letting you build two destinations into one booking. It also draws points from multiple card programs, so it's often reachable whichever currency you hold.
- **Virgin Atlantic Flying Club** is a perennial sweet-spot program for premium-cabin partner awards, and it's a frequent recipient of Amex transfer bonuses, which stretch an already strong rate further.
- **ANA Mileage Club** and **Singapore KrisFlyer** open access to their own excellent long-haul business and first cabins, the kind of seats that are hard to book any other way, at the cost of slower transfers that demand you confirm space first.
- **World of Hyatt** remains the standout hotel transfer, where a modest number of points can cover a room that would cost a great deal in cash, subject to the card-tier ratio caveat above.

No single partner wins every route. The value comes from matching the trip to the program. ## Sweet spots and transfer bonuses

**Transfer bonuses** add another layer. Card issuers periodically run promotions, a 20% or 30% bonus to a given partner, that stretch your points further. They're worth using, with one caveat that returns to the golden rule: a bonus is only a deal if you have a seat to book. Transferring to capture a bonus, with no confirmed award waiting, is speculation dressed up as savings.

The math only works in one direction. A 30% bonus on points you can't redeem is a total loss in disguise, because stranded miles you never use are worth nothing at all. The bonus improves a booking you were already going to make; it does not create one out of thin air.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I transfer airline miles back to my card?

No. Transfers are one-way and, in almost every case, permanent. Once your card points become airline or hotel miles, they belong to that program and follow its rules, its award chart, and its expiration policy. There is no reversal and no path back to your card balance, which is exactly why you confirm the seat before you move anything.

### Do transfer bonuses make speculative transfers worth it?

Rarely, and usually no. A bonus improves a booking you were already going to make; it does not justify moving points with no confirmed award waiting. If the seat you were counting on disappears, a 30% bonus on stranded miles is still a total loss. Use bonuses to sweeten a redemption you can actually complete, not as a reason to transfer speculatively.

### How long do points transfers take?

It depends entirely on the partner. Most airline programs post miles instantly or within a few minutes, which lets you find space and book in one sitting. A handful, notably ANA, Singapore KrisFlyer, and Iberia at the time of writing, can take a day or two. When a transfer is slow, treat the wait as a real risk to your seat and plan around it, or choose an instant partner that prices the same flight.

### Which is the best transfer partner?

There isn't one. The best partner is whichever program prices your specific route in the fewest miles, has a seat available, and transfers at a ratio and speed you can live with. That answer changes trip by trip, which is the whole reason transferable points are valuable.
