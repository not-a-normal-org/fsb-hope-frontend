Transferable points are the most flexible currency in the miles world, and the one beginners misuse most. The mistake is almost always the same: moving points to an airline before there's a seat to book. Transfers are usually irreversible. Get the order wrong and you can strand tens of thousands of points in a program you never meant to use.

This guide explains what transferable points are, how partner transfers actually work, and the single discipline that separates a clean redemption from a costly one.

## What "transferable points" actually means

Five major card currencies are transferable: American Express Membership Rewards, Chase Ultimate Rewards, Citi ThankYou, Capital One Miles, and Bilt Rewards. What makes them powerful is that they don't lock you into one airline. Instead, each one partners with a roster of airline and hotel loyalty programs, and you move points into whichever partner prices your specific flight best.

That flexibility is the whole point. A fixed-value program ties your balance to one airline's award chart. Transferable points let you shop across programs, booking the same physical seat through whichever partner charges the fewest miles. If you're new to the mechanics of earning these points in the first place, start with our [foundation guide to flying on credit card points](/blog/how-to-fly-for-free-with-credit-card-points), then come back here for the transfer step.

## How a transfer works, and the ratios that matter

A transfer moves points out of your card account and into an airline or hotel program as that program's own miles. Most transferable currencies move to their major airline partners at **1:1**: 1,000 card points become 1,000 airline miles. That's the baseline you should expect, and it's why these currencies are valued the way they are.

But 1:1 is not universal, and the exceptions cost real value. At the time of writing:

- **Chase Ultimate Rewards** transfers to every one of its partners at 1:1, in 1,000-point increments.
- **American Express Membership Rewards** is mostly 1:1, but several partners are worse: Cathay Pacific and Emirates transfer at 5:4, JetBlue at roughly 1:0.8, and Hilton at a very different 1:2.
- **Capital One Miles** are largely 1:1, with exceptions such as EVA Air at 4:3.
- **Bilt Rewards** transfers at 1:1 to its airline and hotel partners.
- **Citi ThankYou** ratios depend on which card you hold: premium cards transfer at full value, while some no-annual-fee cards transfer at a reduced 10:7.

Two lessons follow. First, **never assume the ratio**. Check it for your exact card and partner before you move anything, because a 5:4 or 10:7 rate quietly erases 20–30% of your balance. Second, partner rosters and ratios change; treat every list, including this one, as a snapshot to reconfirm at the moment you book.

## The golden rule: find the seat first, then transfer

This is the rule the entire practice rests on: **confirm the award seat is available and bookable before you transfer a single point.**

Award space is finite and volatile. An airline releases a limited number of seats at the saver price, and they can vanish between the moment you see them and the moment you're ready to book. If you transfer speculatively, moving points because a fare *looks* achievable, or because a transfer bonus is ending, you're betting on a seat you haven't secured. When the seat is gone, your points are stranded in an airline program with no easy way back.

The correct sequence is narrow and unforgiving:

1. Find the specific award seat and confirm it's live and bookable.
2. Verify the exact point cost and the transfer ratio for your card.
3. Transfer only the amount that flight requires.
4. Book immediately, while the seat is still there.

Transfers are almost always **one-way and irreversible**. There is no "undo," no customer-service reversal, no transfer back to your card. Once Membership Rewards become airline miles, they follow that airline's rules, its award chart, and its expiration policy, permanently. ## Transfer time is a booking risk, not a footnote

Not all transfers are instant, and speed matters more than people expect. Some partners post the miles in seconds; others take hours, and a few take one to several days.

That lag is a risk, because a seat you confirmed can disappear during a slow transfer. If you find award space with a partner that transfers instantly, you can move points and book in one sitting with confidence. If the partner is a slow one, the seat may not survive the wait, which changes the calculus entirely, and sometimes means choosing a different partner that prices the same flight and transfers instantly.

This is exactly where speculative transfers punish people. Moving points "to be ready" assumes the seat waits for you. It rarely does. The only safe transfer is one made against a seat you've already confirmed, using a partner whose transfer speed you know.

## Sweet spots and transfer bonuses

The reason to learn this machinery is that specific partners price specific routes far below their peers. A well-chosen partner can book the same cabin for a fraction of another program's cost, and these sweet spots are where transferable points earn their reputation. Two familiar examples: some partners let you sidestep punishing carrier-imposed fees, covered in [avoiding fuel surcharges on award flights](/blog/avoid-fuel-surcharges-award-flights), and the right program can put you in a lie-flat seat for a long-haul redemption like [business class to Tokyo on points](/blog/business-class-to-tokyo-with-points).

**Transfer bonuses** add another layer. Card issuers periodically run promotions (a 20% or 30% bonus to a given partner) that stretch your points further. They're worth using, with one caveat that returns to the golden rule: a bonus is only a deal if you have a seat to book. Transferring to capture a bonus, with no confirmed award waiting, is speculation dressed up as savings. Bonuses rotate constantly, so confirm what's live before you count on one.
