**Yes, in most cases you can book an award flight for someone else. United, Delta, American and Southwest let you redeem miles for another traveler, whether or not you fly with them. Two rules decide the rest: banks transfer points only into your own airline account, or an authorized user's in limited cases, and a few programs, including ANA, JAL, Asia Miles and KrisFlyer, ticket only close family or registered nominees.**

## Key takeaways

- Most US airline programs, including United, Delta, American and Southwest, let you redeem miles for anyone, whether or not you are on the flight.
- Card points can only be transferred into an airline account in your own name, with narrow authorized-user exceptions at Chase and Amex, so the usual move is to transfer to yourself and book in the traveler's name.
- ANA, JAL, Asia Miles and KrisFlyer issue awards only to the member and to close family or registered nominees, and Qatar Airways added limits of its own in 2026.
- Check both layers before you move a single point, because transfers to airlines are one-way and an issued award ticket can't be reassigned to another name.

The request usually sounds simple. A parent wants to fly a grown child home for the holidays. A business owner wants to send an employee to a conference on points the company card earned. A couple wants to put a sibling in the seat next to them. In each case the person holding the points is not the person flying, and that works far more often than people expect.

What trips people up is that two different organizations write the rules. The bank decides whose airline account your points may land in. The airline decides whom its miles may be spent on. Get both right and booking for someone else is routine. Get one wrong and the points end up in an account that can't buy the ticket you wanted, with no way back. If you are new to how card points become airline miles, our [guide to flying for free with credit card points](/blog/how-to-fly-for-free-with-credit-card-points) covers the basics first.

## Can you book an award flight for someone else? Usually, yes

Most airline programs let you spend your miles on another person's ticket. Delta says it plainly: you can book award travel for yourself or for someone else, even if you aren't flying with them. United issues MileagePlus award tickets in another person's name, American AAdvantage does the same, and Southwest lets members use Rapid Rewards points to buy seats for themselves or for others.

The mechanics are the same everywhere. You sign in to your own account, pay with your own miles, and enter the traveler as the passenger. The traveler doesn't need a balance of their own, and the fact that you aren't on the flight doesn't matter.

Two limits apply almost everywhere. First, once a ticket is issued, it belongs to the person named on it. Delta's program rules call award tickets nontransferable once issued, and other programs work the same way, so a seat booked for one person can't later be handed to someone else. Second, award tickets are for using, not for selling. Delta prohibits selling or bartering them, and American's terms say the same. Spending your miles on a friend as a gift is fine. Taking payment for the seat is what these rules exist to stop.

## The bank layer: whose airline account your points can reach

This is the rule that catches people, because it applies before you ever see a seat. Every major US card program sends points only into a loyalty account in the cardholder's own name, with two narrow exceptions.

- **Chase Ultimate Rewards:** you can transfer to your own frequent flyer account, or to one authorized user who lives in your household. Once you send points to an authorized user, that person becomes the only one eligible to receive transfers. Chase also lets you combine points with one household member's Chase card that earns Ultimate Rewards.
- **American Express Membership Rewards:** the airline account must be in your name or an Additional Card Member's name, and transfers to an Additional Card Member open once that person's card has been issued for at least 90 days. Amex does not allow family members or friends to share or merge Membership Rewards accounts.
- **Capital One, Citi and Bilt:** the name on the loyalty account has to match the cardholder's name. Capital One does let you move miles to another Capital One cardholder's rewards account by phone, and that person can then transfer them to their own airline account. Citi used to let members share ThankYou points with each other, but it ended points sharing on May 17, 2026.

In practice the cleanest route is almost always the same: transfer the points to your own airline account, then book the seat in the traveler's name from there. It works because the airline rule is looser than the bank rule. It also leaves the miles in an account you control if the plans change.

Whichever route you take, a transfer from a bank to an airline can't be reversed. Chase, Amex, Capital One and Bilt all say so. Our [guide to transfer partners](/blog/transfer-partners-explained-guide) explains why that makes the order of operations so important: confirm the seat first, then move the points.

## The programs that only ticket family or nominees

A handful of programs, several of them among the most useful for premium cabins, won't issue an award to just anyone. They require the traveler to be the member, a close relative, or someone registered on the account in advance.

- **ANA Mileage Club:** awards are for the member, a spouse or same-sex partner, and relatives within two degrees, and each one must be registered as an award user before booking. ANA allows up to 10 registered award users and can ask for proof of the relationship.
- **JAL Mileage Bank:** a similar family-only rule, without the registration step. Award tickets are limited to the member, a spouse or same-sex partner, relatives within two degrees and the spouses of siblings-in-law, and JAL can ask for proof of the relationship.
- **Cathay Pacific Asia Miles:** a member can name up to five nominees, who don't have to be relatives. Awards for a nominee are redeemed by the member on that person's behalf, and anyone not on the list can't be ticketed.
- **Singapore Airlines KrisFlyer:** up to five redemption nominees. Adding one is free, but a nominee can't be removed or replaced for six months, and changes after that cost a fee for most members. A new nominee can only be booked on flights departing more than 72 hours after being added.
- **Qatar Airways Privilege Club:** since June 2026, Qatar has limited award tickets to the member and the people on their My List or Family & Friends group, with extra leeway for Gold and Platinum members. It eased some conditions in September, and the details have changed more than once, so check the current terms before you plan around Qatar for someone else.

The common thread is lead time. These lists take effort to set up, some of them lock once set, and none of them help if the person you want to fly isn't eligible to be on one. If one of these programs is the plan, register or nominate the traveler wherever the program requires it, well before you intend to book, not on the day the seat appears. Our guide to [when award seats open](/blog/when-to-book-award-flights) shows how to work out the first day your program can sell the seat, which is the date to have the traveler registered by.

## When one program says no, book the same seat through another

A family-only rule belongs to the loyalty program, not to the airline's seats. The same Japan Airlines flight that JAL Mileage Bank will only ticket for your relatives can often be booked with American AAdvantage miles instead, and American will issue it to anyone. ANA flights can be booked through United MileagePlus or Air Canada Aeroplan. Cathay Pacific flights can be booked through American or through Alaska's Atmos Rewards.

There are catches. Airlines don't always release the same seats to partners that they sell to their own members, and the partner's price can be higher or lower. Taxes and surcharges differ by program too. Still, when the cheapest program won't ticket your traveler, a second program is usually the answer rather than a dead end. Our post on [Citi points and Japan Airlines](/blog/citi-thankyou-japan-airlines-transfer-partner) shows why American is often the better way into JAL's cabins anyway.

Here is how the two layers combine in one illustrative case. Say you hold Chase points and want to send your adult daughter to Tokyo in business class. Chase can't send points to her United account unless she is an authorized user in your household, so you transfer to your own United account and book her an ANA seat in her name. If the better price is through ANA's own program instead, the bank layer changes, since Chase doesn't transfer to ANA but Amex does, and the airline layer adds a step: as your daughter, she is eligible, but only once she is registered as an award user. Sending an employee instead of a relative on the same trip would rule out ANA's own program entirely, and leave United or Aeroplan, both Chase transfer partners, as the route.

## The details that break a booking for someone else

Most failed bookings for other travelers come down to small details rather than the big rules.

- **The name must match the travel document.** Enter the traveler's name exactly as it appears on the ID or passport they will fly with. Programs may fix a small typo, but none will put a different person on the ticket, and a bigger correction, especially on a partner airline, can mean canceling and rebooking at whatever price is left.
- **The account holder controls the trip.** Southwest's terms say a passenger flying on another member's points can't change the itinerary; the member has to make the changes. Plan on the same elsewhere: changes and cancellations run through the account that paid.
- **Refunds go back to the account that paid, never to the bank.** When an award is canceled, the miles go back to the account that booked it, sometimes for a fee depending on the program and the fare. Because bank transfers are one-way, those miles stay in that airline program for your next trip.
- **Pooling is a different tool.** If several family members each hold some miles, a household or family-pooling program may suit you better, though each comes with its own rules about who can join and who can fly on the pooled miles. Our post on [booking premium seats for a family](/blog/book-premium-seats-for-family-with-points) explains how those work.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use my points to book a flight for someone else?

Usually, yes. United, Delta, American and Southwest all let members redeem miles for other travelers, whether or not the member flies too. If your points sit in a bank program such as Chase or Amex, transfer them to your own airline account first, then book in the traveler's name. The exceptions are a few programs, such as ANA, JAL, Asia Miles and KrisFlyer, that only ticket close family or registered nominees.

### Can I transfer my credit card points to my spouse's airline account?

Only in narrow cases. Chase allows transfers to one authorized user in your household, and Amex allows transfers to an Additional Card Member's account starting 90 days after that card is issued. Capital One, Citi and Bilt require the loyalty account name to match the cardholder's. For most couples the simpler route is to transfer to your own airline account and book your spouse's ticket from there.

### Which airlines only let you book award tickets for family?

The best known are ANA and JAL, which limit awards to the member, a spouse and close relatives (ANA also requires them to be registered in advance), and Cathay Pacific's Asia Miles and Singapore's KrisFlyer, which each allow up to five nominees. Qatar Airways added limits of its own in 2026 and later relaxed some of them. You can often book the same flights through a partner program without such a rule, such as American AAdvantage for JAL.

### If I book an award for someone else and it's canceled, where do the miles go?

Back to the airline account that paid for the ticket, which is yours, not the traveler's. Depending on the program and the fare, a cancellation fee may apply. The miles never return to Chase, Amex or any other bank, because transfers from a bank to an airline are one-way. That is a good reason to confirm the trip and the seat before moving points for someone else.
