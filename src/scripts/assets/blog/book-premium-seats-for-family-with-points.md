One business class seat to Europe is a solvable puzzle. Four seats on the same flight, in the same cabin, on the dates a family can actually travel, is a different order of difficulty. This is the request that breaks most do-it-yourself searches, and it is the one people most want to get right, because it is usually a once-a-year trip with children in tow.

The good news is that it is regularly done. It just rewards planning, flexibility, and knowing which levers to pull, in what order. Here is how a family should think about it.

## Why two to four premium seats are genuinely scarce

Airlines release award seats in premium cabins sparingly. A long-haul aircraft might have thirty-odd business class seats, and the carrier would rather sell them for cash. What it releases to the loyalty program is a small slice, and that slice is often one or two seats at a time. Finding four together, on one flight, is not a matter of searching harder. Frequently the inventory simply is not there on the day you look.

The scale of the problem is well documented. An analysis of long-haul business class award bookings by ANA found that the large majority of successful ones were made roughly nine months or more before departure, precisely because so few seats open up closer in. Multiple seats compound that: each additional traveler cuts the number of flights that can hold your whole party.

Two facts follow from this, and both are good news. First, the seats do exist. They are released, just thinly and unpredictably. Second, you rarely need all of them from a single source. The craft is in assembling them.

## Book far ahead, or pounce on the schedule change

There are two reliable windows for multiple premium seats.

The first is the moment an airline's schedule opens, typically around eleven to twelve months before departure. This is when the fullest, freshest inventory appears, and it is the best single shot at four seats on one flight. It also means committing to dates before life has fully firmed up.

The second window is less obvious: schedule changes. When an airline adjusts its timetable, re-times a flight, or swaps aircraft, it often dumps fresh award space back into inventory, sometimes months after the initial release, when everyone assumes the flight is closed. Watching for these releases is tedious and continuous, which is exactly why they are so often missed by someone checking once a week.

If you would rather understand the machinery behind these releases first, our companion piece on [what a specialist does when there's no award space](/blog/no-award-space-what-a-specialist-does) walks through it.

## Split across programs and partners

Here is the single most useful idea for family award travel: the same physical seat can be booked through several different loyalty programs, and each program shows its own availability and charges its own price.

A flight that looks empty through the operating airline's own program may show two seats through an alliance partner, and two more through a transfer currency that aggregates partner space. Booking two travelers on one program and two on another, for the same flight, is a standard, legitimate technique. So is accepting that your four seats come as two separate reservations rather than one.

This is also where knowing the transfer landscape pays off. Flexible points from a card program can often be moved to whichever airline currency happens to show the space. Our [guide to transfer partners](/blog/transfer-partners-explained-guide) covers how those pathways work; the short version is that the more currencies a family can reach, the more doors it can try on the same flight.

## Pool points, and use several currencies at once

A family rarely needs to fund four premium seats from one person's balance. Two mechanics make this practical.

Pooling lets household members combine balances. British Airways runs a Household Account that lets you pool Avios with up to seven other people, where any member can redeem the shared pot for themselves or for named family and friends. Air Canada's Aeroplan offers Family Sharing for up to eight members, where everyone's points flow into a single pool at no fee and the primary member can book for anyone in the group. These are official, free programs, not workarounds, and they turn scattered balances into one usable total. (Program rules change; we verify the current terms before building a plan around them.)

Beyond formal pooling, a family can simply run parallel currencies: one parent's points cover two seats through one airline, the other parent's points cover two through another. Different currencies, same flight, one trip.

## The compromises that actually get everyone on the plane

When four seats in one cabin refuse to appear, these are the trade-offs seasoned bookers reach for, in rough order of preference:

1. **Nearby-date flexibility.** Shifting departure by a day or two, or flying midweek, is the highest-yield lever. Award calendars swing wildly day to day.
2. **Mixed cabin.** Two adults in business, two children in premium economy on the same flight is a common, comfortable compromise, and far cheaper in points.
3. **Split flights.** Two travelers on the morning departure, two on the evening one, meeting at the destination. Not ideal with small children, but it works.
4. **Positioning flights.** A short, cheap hop to a city with better award space (say, flying to a hub where four seats open up) can unlock an otherwise impossible long-haul leg.
5. **Split routings.** Different connecting cities that rejoin at the destination.

None of these is a failure. They are the normal shape of a real family award booking, and a good plan sequences them so you reach for flexibility before you spend more points.

## What a family should realistically expect to pay

Point costs move, so treat these as evergreen ranges rather than live prices. For a round trip in a premium cabin, per person:

- **Transatlantic (US to Europe), business class:** typically in the range of 100,000 to 160,000 points round trip, plus cash surcharges that vary sharply by program.
- **Transpacific or US to Asia, business class:** typically 150,000 to 250,000 points round trip.
- **First class, where available:** often 200,000 to 400,000-plus points per person, and genuinely scarce for multiple seats.

For a family of four in business, plan around a multiple of those figures, and expect the real cost to depend heavily on which programs you can reach and how much you avoid high surcharges. ## Where a human earns the fee

A solo search checks a few programs, on a few dates, once. A specialist checks thirty-plus programs by hand, cross-references partner availability, watches for schedule-change releases over weeks, and knows which currency prices a given seat lowest with the smallest surcharge. For one seat, that edge is nice. For four seats together, it is often the difference between the trip happening and not.
