Award-search tools have changed how people find point flights. A decade ago, checking a single route across a handful of programs meant loading airline sites one by one. Now a tool can sweep a year of dates across dozens of programs in seconds. That is real progress, and it is worth crediting plainly.

But finding *availability* and holding a *ticket* are two different things. The tools are very good at the first. The gap between the two is where a human specialist spends most of the work. This is a fair look at both: what the software genuinely does well, and what still needs a person.

## What the tools do well

Automated award search is fast, broad, and improving. Used well, these tools save hours.

- **[Seats.aero](https://thepointsguy.com/travel/seats-aero/)** scans a wide date range across roughly fifteen programs in one pass, with strong filters for date, origin and point cost, and a long forward search window for hard-to-find premium seats.
- **[Point.me](https://thepointsparty.com/articles/best-award-search-engines)** covers 30+ loyalty programs and pairs results with clear transfer instructions, which makes it approachable for people newer to award travel.
- **Roame** is known for a clean interface and a discovery view that surfaces recently found space.
- **ExpertFlyer** reads real airline inventory classes and sets availability alerts, useful for confirming whether a seat is genuinely open.
- **Google Flights with the Points Path extension** overlays approximate point pricing onto ordinary cash-flight search, a low-friction way to spot when points might beat cash.

If your goal is to see, quickly, whether award space *appears* to exist on a route, these tools are an excellent starting point. Coverage and speed are exactly what software is good at.

## Where "shows availability" stops

A search result is a snapshot of a database, not a promise. Award inventory is volatile. Space can appear in an aggregator's cache and be gone by the time you open the airline site, and phantom space, seats a program advertises but will not actually confirm, is common enough that it is [its own well-known problem](/blog/phantom-award-space-why-seats-vanish).

Verifying that a specific seat is *bookable, today, for the passengers you have* is manual work. It means going to the operating program, holding the segment, and confirming the price the tool quoted is the price the airline honors. A tool can tell you a seat was seen. Only a check confirms it is real.

## Judging the tradeoffs, not just the price

Award tools sort by point cost. That number is one input, not the decision.

The same seat can often be booked through several different programs, each with its own price, its own transfer partners, and its own quirks. One partner may add heavy fuel surcharges that quietly double the cash you pay at booking; another may price the identical flight in miles you can reach through a better transfer bonus. A specialist weighs:

- Which program to book *through*, given the points you actually hold or can transfer.
- Fuel surcharges and carrier fees the headline point number hides.
- Routing rules (married segments, permitted connections, stopovers) that decide whether a promising itinerary can be ticketed at all.
- Cabin consistency across segments, so you are not paying business-class miles for an economy leg in the middle.

These are judgment calls across competing options. Tools present the options well. Choosing among them, for your points and your trip, is where a person earns their place.

## The tool doesn't know your points

An award engine searches the world's availability. It does not know your wallet. It cannot see that your Amex balance is one transfer bonus away from covering a seat, that your Chase points reach a partner the tool never surfaced, or that a currency you already hold prices this exact flight better than the "cheapest" result on screen. It sorts by an abstract point number, not by what *you* can actually spend.

A specialist starts from the other end: what you hold, across every card and program, and what those specific balances can reach. The same seat that looks unaffordable at a tool's headline rate can be well within range once the search is anchored to your real currencies and the transfer ratios that apply to your cards. That is a different question than "where is award space," and it is the one that decides whether a trip is bookable for the person in front of us, not for a hypothetical searcher with a blank slate of points.

## Complex trips break the pattern

Single traveler, one seat, simple round trip: the tools handle that case beautifully. The work compounds when the trip does.

Two, four, or five passengers on the same cabin is a different problem, because programs rarely release multiple premium seats on the same flight, and space that exists for one traveler may not exist for a family. Multi-city routings, open jaws, mixed cabins, and positioning flights multiply the combinations past what a filter comfortably expresses. This is the situation where a specialist [pieces together a bookable itinerary by hand](/blog/no-award-space-what-a-specialist-does) when the obvious search comes back empty, testing alternate dates, nearby airports, and split bookings until the pieces fit.

## The booking itself

Finding the seat is not the last step. Points sit in the wrong place until you move them, and transfers are often *irreversible*. Send miles to the wrong program, or transfer before confirming the seat still exists, and you can strand points you can't get back.

A human specialist sequences this carefully: confirm the seat is live, verify the exact transfer ratio and any timing lag, move the points, and ticket before the space moves. If something shifts mid-process, and award space shifts, a person adapts in real time. That hand-holding, from "here is your seat" to "you are holding a ticket," is the part no dashboard completes for you. ## Tools are the start; a person closes the gap

None of this is a knock on the software. Seats.aero, Point.me, Roame, ExpertFlyer and Points Path are genuinely useful, and a lot of good trips begin inside them. The honest framing is this: they show you where availability *might* be.
