**Work out the first date your own program can sell the seat, then plan around that rather than around the airline's schedule. Award booking opens between 331 and 361 days before departure where a program publishes a limit at all, and several of the largest publish nothing. Saver space then arrives in two clusters, once at schedule open and again in the final weeks, and which cluster matters depends on the airline.**

## Key takeaways

- The first bookable date belongs to the program spending the miles, not to the airline flying the plane, so the same seat can be for sale in one program weeks before another program's calendar reaches it.
- Several major programs, Delta, Alaska's Atmos Rewards, Flying Blue and Avianca LifeMiles among them, publish no maximum advance window in their award rules at all, which means the day counts repeated across the internet come from award-search vendors rather than from the airline.
- Qatar Airways states in its Privilege Club terms that its award time limits vary by travel date, booking date and membership tier, so there is no single number to put in your calendar.
- Close-in award space can be gone within hours, and Chase says point transfers usually land the next business day but may take up to seven, so the binding constraint is often how fast your points move rather than whether you find the seat.

Almost every guide to award booking gives the same instruction: book the day the calendar opens, roughly eleven months out. It is right for some airlines, useless for others, and it skips the question that actually decides your first move, which is when your own program can sell you the ticket.

Two different things get called a release. The airline loads a schedule, which makes the flight exist. Separately, revenue management decides how many seats to sell at the saver award price, and it keeps revisiting that decision until the door closes. The first has a date you can calculate. The second is a pattern that varies by airline more than any rule admits.

## The first bookable date belongs to your program

Subtract your program's advance limit from your departure date and you have the first morning worth looking. Take a departure on December 18, 2027. British Airways Club, whose [terms](https://www.britishairways.com/content/the-british-airways-club/about-the-club/terms-and-conditions) put reward bookings at up to 355 days before departure, reaches that date on December 28, 2026. American AAdvantage, which states 331 days, cannot sell it until January 21, 2027. That is 24 days in which the seat exists, shows up in award search tools, and cannot be bought with the miles in your account. People read that gap as phantom space. It is a calendar they are not in yet.

Where programs do publish a limit in their award rules, the numbers are these. American AAdvantage states 331 days. Qantas puts Classic Flight Rewards at 353. British Airways Club says 355. [ANA](https://www.ana.co.jp/en/us/amc/international-flight-awards) opens at 355 days from 09:00 Japan time, which its own page helpfully translates to 4:00 pm Pacific for US members. [Finnair Plus](https://www.finnair.com/en/finnair-plus/finnair-plus-rules) says award flights can be booked no earlier than 360 days out. [Miles and More](https://www.miles-and-more.com/row/en/general-information/terms-and-conditions/flight-awards.html) states 361.

Two of those deserve a note. Finnair is widely listed as 361 and one major guide says 331, while Finnair's own rules say 360. Miles and More is almost universally listed as 360, and Lufthansa's own terms say 361. When the trackers and the program disagree, the program's own award rules are the ones that govern your booking.

Now the part that is rarely said out loud. Several of the biggest programs publish no maximum award window anywhere. Delta's award pages give minimum advance rules and capacity controls but no ceiling; the 331 days attached to Delta is a Delta Vacations packages figure. Alaska's 330 days is a statement about its paid Premium Class cabin, not about Atmos Rewards awards. Air France-KLM frames Flying Blue as bookable "until the last available seat" and gives no day count. Avianca publishes only a 48-hour minimum for LifeMiles. Qatar goes further and says explicitly, in clause 13.7 of its [Privilege Club terms](https://www.qatarairways.com/en-us/Privilege-Club/terms-and-conditions.html), that its award time limits vary by date of travel, date of booking and membership tier.

United is the sharpest example of why this matters. Award trackers list it at 337 days with striking unanimity. United's own site says you can book an award up to 331 days before departure on most carriers. Both numbers are in circulation, only one of them is United's, and the difference is six days at the exact moment the good seats go.

So treat every day count you read, including the ones above, as a starting point to confirm in the account you will actually book from. Where the program publishes nothing, the number in the tables is someone's observation, which is useful and is not a rule.

One more trap, because it breaks the arithmetic rather than bending it: ANA counts the window from the return flight on a round trip, since the itinerary is booked and ticketed together. Your outbound being inside the window is not enough.

## Release timing differs by airline more than any rule admits

Beyond the calendar, there is the question of when the saver seats actually appear, and here the public evidence is thin. The most detailed figures come from a data study by AwardClaw, an award-alert service, which reports tracking 55,703 saver business and first class seats since July 20, 2026 and logging how many days before departure each first appeared. Its medians put EVA Air business class at 4 days before departure, ANA at 43, American at 121, Turkish at 191, Japan Airlines at 198 and Qatar at 294.

Read that as a direction of travel, not as measurement, for three reasons that the study's own pages supply. It covers eight loyalty programs, not the 48 airlines the headline counts, because an airline is only visible through a program that can book it. Its about page says availability is scanned every three hours with a four-hour overnight pause, while the study page says hourly. And it is an alert service publishing evidence that seats are hard to catch without alerts.

The third problem is the interesting one, because it proves the earlier section. Finnair's median first sighting lands at 331 days, which is not a fact about Finnair. It is American AAdvantage's booking window, and American is one of only two programs in the study that can see Finnair at all. The study is partly measuring when its own search calendar opens. That is the program-window effect, visible in the data of someone who did not set out to demonstrate it, and it is a good reason to work out your own first bookable date rather than trusting a chart of when seats "appear".

What survives independent checking is the shape rather than the numbers: some airlines load most saver space when the schedule opens, others release it close in, and no single instruction covers both. Qatar's own guidance to book at the opening hour is widely corroborated. EVA Air's late pattern is reported elsewhere, though thinly. Treat anything more precise as a lead to verify on your route.

## The late window is a different kind of booking

Catching space in the final weeks is a different task from catching it at schedule open. Premium saver space can vanish in hours: Mainly Miles, tracking Singapore Airlines award loads, recorded six saver business seats on one flight disappearing in under three hours, and saver counts on US routes falling by 60 to 75 percent within the first week of release.

That changes what your points have to be able to do. Transfers are one way and cannot be undone, which is why the standing rule is never to move points before a seat is confirmed, as our [transfer partners guide](/blog/transfer-partners-explained-guide) sets out. They are also slower than their reputation. Chase says most transfers process by the next business day but may take up to seven business days. Marriott's terms say miles are posted to the member's account within one week in most cases. A transfer that lands after the seat is gone has bought you nothing, and it cannot be sent back.

There is no clever way around that tension, only an informed choice. For the late window, favour programs you can reach quickly from the currency you hold, decide in advance what you would accept so you are not deliberating while the seat ages, and treat anything needing a slow transfer as an early-window target. This is the practical difference between space you can see and space you can buy, which is [phantom award space](/blog/phantom-award-space-why-seats-vanish) arriving by another route.

## Check that the cabin is sold to partners at all

Some plans fail before any of this applies, because the seat is never offered to the program you hold points in. Lufthansa first class is the standing example. Partner programs could once book it up to about 15 days out; reporting since early 2024 describes that narrowing to three or four days, and through the second half of 2026 one tracker logged no partner-bookable saver first class on Lufthansa at any distance from departure. Lufthansa publishes no partner release rule, so every figure in circulation is an observation, and the observations have been getting worse. SWISS first class has been restricted to its own top-tier members for years.

The lesson generalises past Lufthansa. Before you build a year of planning on a cabin, confirm that your program can sell it, rather than confirming only that the airline flies it.

## A plan for a trip you have not booked yet

For a trip twelve months out, the sequence that fits both windows looks like this:

1. Fix the two things that do not move: roughly when you want to travel, and which airports you would genuinely accept at each end.
2. Decide which program would issue the ticket, then find its advance limit in its own award rules, not in a chart, and calculate the opening date from your departure date.
3. Hold your points in the transferable currency rather than the airline program. Nothing about early planning requires an early transfer, and the [valuation math in our guide to flying on credit card points](/blog/how-to-fly-for-free-with-credit-card-points) is easier to apply while you still have the choice.
4. Look on the opening day. If the space is there, book it and stop.
5. If it is not, switch from searching to watching, and accept that the answer may not arrive until the final month.
6. Keep a second route and a second program live. [When nothing is available](/blog/no-award-space-what-a-specialist-does), bending the trip is usually what works, not searching harder.

The step people skip is the third, and it is the expensive one. Points moved into an airline program in January for a December trip sit there through every schedule change and devaluation in between.

## Frequently asked questions

### How far in advance can I book an award flight?

Where the program publishes a limit, between 331 and 361 days before departure: American states 331, Qantas 353, British Airways and ANA 355, Finnair 360 and Miles and More 361. Several large programs, including Delta, Atmos Rewards, Flying Blue and LifeMiles, publish no maximum at all, and Qatar says its limits vary by tier and booking date. Check the account you will book from.

### Is it better to book award flights early or wait?

It depends on the airline, and the honest answer is that you often cannot know in advance which pattern yours follows. Qatar's premium space is widely reported to go at the opening hour, while EVA Air's tends to appear within days of departure. The low-cost move is to look on your program's opening day, then keep watching rather than concluding the trip is impossible.

### Why can I see the flight but not book it with my miles?

Usually because your program's booking calendar has not reached that date yet. A flight that opened 355 days ahead is visible in award search tools immediately, but a program with a 331-day window cannot sell it for another 24 days. The space is real and may well survive. Note the date your program opens and come back then.

### How long does award space last once it appears?

Sometimes weeks, sometimes hours. Tracking of Singapore Airlines award releases recorded saver business seats on one flight gone in under three hours, and saver counts falling 60 to 75 percent in the first week after release. Where space disappears that fast, the practical question is not how often you search but whether your points can move before it goes.
