**Rank the major hotel programs by the largest transfer bonus each has ever received and you get almost exactly the inverse of what their points are worth.** Hilton has reached 200 percent, IHG 150, Marriott 70. World of Hyatt, the most valuable of the four, has never received a transfer bonus from any issuer. A bonus is a discount, and the program everybody already wants has never needed to offer one.

## Key takeaways

- Among hotel programs the pattern is strikingly consistent: the deeper the discount, the weaker the currency, and the strongest program has never discounted at all.
- Score any offer in cents per bank point, not as a percentage, because base ratios differ and a percentage hides them.
- Done that way, headline bonuses converge: a 200 percent offer into a weak program and a 70 percent offer into a middling one both land near 1.2 cents, which an undiscounted transfer into a strong program beats.
- The pattern does not extend to airlines. Some issuers rotate large bonuses across their whole airline roster as monthly marketing, so a big airline bonus says little about the program receiving it.

Every few weeks a list appears: the transfer bonuses live right now, ranked by percentage. The lists are accurate and nearly useless, because percentage is the one property of a bonus that does not survive contact with arithmetic.

Start with the fact that should make you suspicious of the whole category.

## The program that has never discounted

Published valuations put World of Hyatt points somewhere between 1.4 and 1.8 cents depending on the publisher, Marriott Bonvoy around 0.7 to 0.75, IHG around 0.6, and Hilton Honors between roughly 0.35 and 0.6. That is the ranking.

Now the bonus history. Hilton has been the target of offers advertised as high as 200 percent. IHG has seen 150, and 100 more than once. Marriott has repeatedly been bonused at 50, 65 and 70 percent, several times in a single year. Hyatt has never been on the receiving end of a transfer bonus from any issuer, in the entire history of the program.

Four programs, and the size of the discount runs precisely opposite to the value of the points.

## Why that is, mechanically

A transfer bonus is a volume promotion. Issuers and programs run them to move points that are not moving on their own, and the programs that discount deepest and most often are the ones that find their currency hardest to shift. The one program everybody already wants has never had to.

Read that way, a large bonus is information, and the information is rarely flattering. It is the loyalty equivalent of a permanent sale rack.

Experienced commentators write about these offers in exactly those terms. The coverage of the deepest hotel bonuses tends toward "give this a miss" rather than excitement, and the reason is the arithmetic below rather than snobbery.

## Score it in cents per bank point

The fix is one calculation. Take the base transfer ratio, apply the bonus, multiply by what one partner point is realistically worth, and you have the value of one of *your* points through that door. Percentages cannot be compared across partners because the base ratios differ, and the bank point is the only unit you actually spend.

Run the ladder, using mid-range published valuations:

- A **200 percent** bonus into the weakest major hotel program lands around **1.2 cents** per bank point.
- A **100 percent** bonus into the next weakest lands around **1.2 cents**.
- A **70 percent** bonus into the middling one lands around **1.28 cents**.
- A plain **1:1 transfer into the strongest, with no bonus at all**, lands around **1.4 to 1.65 cents**.

The headline numbers span 200 percent to zero. The outcomes span about four tenths of a cent, and the best of them carries no bonus. That is the finding worth taking away: the deepest discounts land on currencies that still lose to an ordinary transfer into a good program.

One live caveat on that last line. Chase and Bilt both cut their Hyatt ratios to 4:3 for most cards during 2026, which pulls that door down to roughly 1.05 to 1.35 cents for affected cardholders and genuinely narrows the gap. We worked through what to do about it in [the Hyatt transfer deadline](/blog/hyatt-transfer-deadline-what-to-do).

## The exception that proves it is about the destination

There is a real counterexample and it is instructive rather than awkward. Accor's currency is valued unusually highly, near the top of the hotel table, and it has still been the subject of very large bonuses. Score one of those in cents per bank point and it comes out far above everything in the ladder above.

So the rule is not "big bonuses are bad". It is that the bonus tells you almost nothing and the destination tells you almost everything. When a deep discount lands on a genuinely strong currency, it is the best offer on the page. That combination is simply rare, and it is rare for the mechanical reason given above.

## Why percentages mislead twice over

Two details make headline numbers worse than useless if you compare them directly.

**Base ratios differ.** Some programs transfer at 1:1, some at 1:2, some at 3:2. A bonus is applied on top of whatever the base is, so the same percentage produces wildly different quantities depending on the pair.

**The biggest numbers are usually conditional.** The deepest advertised hotel bonuses have tended to be "up to" figures, reserved for a top elite tier, capped at a certain number of points, available for a single day, and sometimes requiring additional spending to unlock. Comparing a conditional ceiling against another issuer's standing offer is not a comparison at all.

## The bonus does not touch the risk

A bonus changes the quantity on one side of an irreversible trade. It changes nothing else about the trade.

Transfers are one way, and no major issuer reverses them. The award still has to exist on your dates. The program can still reprice its chart after your points land. The partner can still stop selling what you wanted, which is not hypothetical: [an entire partner relationship went dark](/blog/when-a-partner-stops-selling-awards) this October with no explanation and no stated end date.

A 40 percent bonus on miles you never use is not a 40 percent gain. It is a total loss with a bigger number attached. Our [transfer partners guide](/blog/transfer-partners-explained-guide) lays out the sequence that prevents this, and a promotion is not a reason to break it.

## The three questions, in order

**1. What is it worth in cents per bank point?** Do the arithmetic above. If it does not clear what an ordinary transfer into a program you rate would give you, stop. Nothing else about the offer matters.

**2. Do I have a booking, or an intention?** A booking means specific dates, a confirmed award, and a known cost in points and cash. An intention is a plan to look later. The first justifies a transfer and the second does not.

**3. Would I transfer without it?** If the honest answer is no, the bonus is not improving your plan, it is supplying one.

## When it genuinely is worth taking

The strict line is to transfer only against a confirmed booking, and that is the right default. It is worth saying that the field is not unanimous: some respected commentators will transfer speculatively when a large bonus lands on a program they are confident they will use, and they name the strong programs when they do it. Nobody credible argues for speculative transfers into the weak ones, which is the same finding from the other side.

So the honest version of the exception is narrow:

- You use the receiving program **regularly** and have a record of redeeming there.
- The cents-per-bank-point figure **clears** what you would otherwise get, which usually means a strong destination rather than a big percentage.
- You can **absorb being wrong**, and you move a portion rather than a whole balance.

Topping up is the cleaner case. If you are a few thousand points short of a specific booking, a bonus makes closing that gap cheaper than buying points outright, and the risk is near zero because the booking already exists. Watch the transfer minimums and increments, which often mean you cannot move the exact amount and will strand a remainder.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are transfer bonuses worth it?

Only when they improve a redemption you were already going to make. A bonus increases how many partner points you receive and changes nothing about whether the award exists, whether the program will reprice it, or whether you can get your points back, which you cannot. Convert the offer into cents per bank point, compare it against an ordinary transfer into a program you actually rate, and treat anything that fails that comparison as a promotion rather than an opportunity, whatever the percentage.

### Why are hotel transfer bonuses so much larger than airline ones?

Among hotel programs the size of a bonus tracks the weakness of the currency, because a bonus is a volume promotion and the programs that discount deepest are the ones whose points move least readily. The most valuable hotel program has never needed one. Be careful extending that logic to airlines: some issuers rotate large bonuses across their entire airline roster as regular marketing, including to programs the community rates highly, so an airline bonus carries much less information about the destination.

### Should I transfer points speculatively during a bonus?

The default answer is no, because a speculative transfer takes on availability risk, devaluation risk and irreversibility, and a bonus offsets none of them. The honest exception, which some experienced commentators do take, is a large bonus into a program you demonstrably use and rate, where you move a portion of a balance rather than all of it. Nobody credible recommends speculative transfers into the weakest programs, which is the same conclusion arrived at from the other direction.

### How do I compare a transfer bonus with using points as cash?

Work out the cents per bank point each way. For the bonus, apply the base ratio and the bonus, then multiply by a realistic value for one partner point. For the cash route, a portal usually pays a fixed rate per point, which makes it the easy side of the comparison. The transfer wins when the specific award you have found beats that fixed rate by enough to justify giving up flexibility, which some premium-cabin awards do and many ordinary redemptions do not.
